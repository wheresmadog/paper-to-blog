# Conference poster layout

Use this guide for a poster that supports both a quick walk-by reading and a longer discussion. User templates and verified venue rules take precedence.

## Default canvas and hierarchy

For the generic wide landscape poster (1200 × 600 mm), start from the bundled template: a compact full-width header followed by six modular panels in two rows. Its card dimensions and grid spans are initial layout choices, not fixed requirements. Adjust widths, heights, row proportions, and panel count to the paper's content while retaining the template's visual style and the overall canvas size. Enlarge a method or evidence panel when its figure needs space; reduce, merge, or split supporting panels as useful. Use modest margins and consistent gutters so content occupies the canvas without crowding.

For vertical posters, use [the portrait template](../assets/poster-template-portrait.html): an A0 canvas (841 × 1189 mm), with three cards in each of two columns. Card heights vary independently in each column, producing staggered boundaries that illustrate flexible sizing. Guide reading with section numbers from the top toward the bottom. Adapt card sizes and spans as in landscape; a wide figure can occupy a full-width panel. The portrait template retains the same typography, blue heading bars, rounded outlines, and local assets, with a centered stacked footer.

A useful starting typography range at this physical size is 60–80 pt for the title, 30–38 pt for section headings, 22–28 pt for body copy, and 20–24 pt for captions. These are design starting points, not conference requirements. Adapt them to the dimensions and inspect a rendered preview. Embedded figure labels must remain legible too; large surrounding text cannot rescue a tiny plot legend.

Establish three levels of attention:

1. Title and takeaway: what question is addressed and what was learned?
2. Main visual and result: what mechanism or evidence supports the answer?
3. Supporting details: what settings, assumptions, and limits make the result interpretable?

Use a white background, dark text, blue heading bars with white centered text, thin blue rounded outlines, and a small set of accent colors unless the user specifies branding. Encode comparisons with labels or line styles as well as colors. Aim for a logical reading path rather than symmetrical boxes of equal importance.

## Content regions

### Header

Keep the paper title, author order, and affiliations accurate. A concise reader-facing subtitle or takeaway can supplement the title. Place verified logos or a paper link where they do not compete with the main message. Do not add a conference badge that implies acceptance.

### Problem and contribution

Use a brief statement of the practical or scientific problem, the missing capability, and what this paper contributes. Prefer one concrete example over a long related-work section. Define only the background needed to understand the rest.

### Method or central argument

Let a source diagram, readable equation, or short pipeline carry the explanation. Explain inputs, the key operation, and outputs with concise labels. For a theory paper, present the intuitive statement and assumptions; for a survey, use a field map or organizing taxonomy.

### Evidence

Give the largest useful visual space to the central result. A comparison must identify the task, baseline, metric, and whether higher or lower is better. Retain error bars or uncertainty when available. If a source table is too dense, select a justified subset and label the scope; do not silently remove results that weaken the takeaway.

Do not transform agreement with a reference calculation into experimental validation, or an illustrative example into a benchmark. When the paper supplies no quantitative evaluation, make that clear and organize the poster around the actual argument or artifact.

### Takeaway and limits

End with a concise supported conclusion and the most consequential boundary or open question. Keep limits visible beside the results rather than hiding them in a tiny footer. The poster should remain honest when read without an accompanying presenter.

### Footer

Provide a compact paper citation and versioned URL, plus a small number of essential references if needed. Add project links or contact information only when supplied or verified. Do not fabricate a repository or email to fill empty space.

## Figure handling

Usually select two to four strong visuals, or fewer when the paper has only one informative figure. A wide central figure can be more useful than several unreadably small panels. Figure count follows the research and canvas, not a quota.

Prefer source image assets or high-resolution PDF rendering. Check effective resolution at the printed size and inspect fine labels; never upscale a small bitmap and describe it as high-resolution. Preserve aspect ratios and provide short source-aware captions. For a complex multi-panel figure, a selected panel must remain interpretable and be labeled as a selection from the original figure.

## Print and screen verification

The print layout has a physical canvas; the screen preview may scale or scroll that canvas. Responsive screen styles must not change the print dimensions or split columns across pages. Verify print layout with the actual exporter or print preview, since browser CSS support varies.

Inspect the whole poster for hierarchy and reading order, then inspect title, captions, legends, and dense content at detailed scale. In the exported PDF, check fonts, image quality, one-page output, dimensions, and the absence of accidental headers, footers, or cropped edges.
